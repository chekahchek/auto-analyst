What actually drove the doubling of store sales in Ecuador from 2013 to 2017 and how much of that growth is real?

Subject
Daily store-level sales, 54 supermarkets, Ecuador
Period
1 Jan 2013 – 15 Aug 2017
Unit
Units sold, reported per store, per day, per product family
Headline
Average daily sales 386K → 791K units, +106% (2013–2016)
Attribution
New stores +17 of those points · the 47 incumbents +88
Bottom line
The doubling is real, and it happened inside stores that were already open. Promotions explain why the headline is bigger than the demand; the oil-price link explains nothing at all.

How to read the stamps

  • Real — durable demand
  • Baseline — a rhythm you can predict
  • Shock — one-off, and it fades
  • Amplifier — inflates the headline
  • Artifact — spurious, not a signal

F–01

Same-store spread

Real

Growth is real — but it was never one tide lifting all boats

Two supermarkets. Same chain, same country, same three years. Store 36 grew 239%. Store 26 grew 19%. Every one of the 47 stores open on day one grew — but the gap between best and worst is 12x.

That spread is the first clue that the chain’s headline growth number is an average hiding several very different stories underneath it.

F–02

Where the volume sits

Real

Chain-wide, sales roughly doubled — and new stores barely explain it

Average daily sales rose from 386K units in 2013 to 791K in 2016, +106%. Only 17% of that increase came from new stores; the 47 incumbents delivered +88% on their own.

It is also a concentrated business: the top 10 of 54 stores generate 40% of volume, and the top 5 of 33 product families generate 79% — GROCERY I alone accounts for 32%. Between-store differences explain 74% of total sales variance versus 26% for change over time, so who the stores are matters more than the trend. If the action sits inside existing stores, what is moving demand week to week?

F–03

The calendar

Baseline

The calendar does a lot of the work

Weekends run about 39% above weekdays — Sunday averages 825K units against Thursday’s 505K — and December averages 27% above the year-round daily mean.

With one telling exception: at store 1 weekends are BELOW weekdays (ratio 0.70), the signature of a commuter or office-district location rather than a neighbourhood store. Predictable rhythms like these form the baseline — against which shocks stand out.

F–04

A one-off shock

Shock

The single largest demand shock: a 7.8-magnitude earthquake

The week of 16–22 April 2016 totalled 9.6M units against 5.1M the week before, +87%. Monday 18 April alone hit 1.35M units — 2.18x a normal Monday.

In the short run this series is moved by stockpiling and events, not by steady demand — a reminder not to read too much into any single month of the topline. But shocks fade. Something more durable was also quietly changing.

F–05

Promotional intensity

Amplifier

Promotions scaled from nothing to nearly half of all sales

Promotion coverage rose from 0% of rows in 2013 to 45% by 2017. Within the same product family, promoted rows sell 1.4–3.1x more (median ~1.6x): PRODUCE 3.1x, BEVERAGES 2.5x, DAIRY 2.0x.

Because promotional coverage ramped up in step with the sales trend, part of the multi-year ‘growth’ reflects promotional intensity rather than pure organic demand. This is the complication the +106% headline quietly absorbs.

F–06

A macro red herring

Artifact

The oil-price story looks compelling — until you detrend it

Daily sales correlate −0.70 with the daily oil price. It reads like oil is driving demand; it isn’t. Oil simply fell from 110 to 26 USD while sales rose. Strip out the shared trend and the correlation collapses to +0.07.

There is a practical catch too: oil prices exist on only 1,163 of 1,684 days, because commodity markets are closed at weekends and on holidays — so weekend sales have no same-day oil value at all. Any model built on this pairing is largely modelling the calendar.

Finding of fact

What actually drove the doubling

Sales roughly doubled between 2013 and 2016, but the growth came overwhelmingly from stores that were already open — incumbents grew +88% while new openings added just 17% of the increase — and it was amplified by promotions rising from 0% to 45% of rows. On top of that sits a demand pattern dominated by the calendar (weekends +39%, December +27%) and punctuated by one-off shocks such as the April 2016 earthquake.

The apparent oil-price link is an artifact of two independent trends moving in opposite directions, not a demand signal. The practical implication: judge this business on same-store performance and promotional intensity, treat macro correlations as guilty until proven innocent, and de-duplicate the data before measuring anything — otherwise 3.1% duplicate rows silently inflate the total by 2.1%.

Appendix

Records, duplicates, gaps

Methodology and data caveats

Daily store-level sales for 54 Ecuadorian stores, 1 Jan 2013 – 15 Aug 2017 (1,684 dates). 3.1% of rows (96,228) are duplicate store-date-family records created by overlapping event labels; de-duplicating on date × store × family cuts the raw total by 2.1%. About 10 non-core families report exactly zero sales chain-wide for whole months in 2014 and early 2015 (85 family-months in total), producing artificial dips in the topline that make those category trends unreliable before mid-2015. Seven stores are pre-opening placeholders sitting at zero until they open, and Christmas Day is absent in all four years. New-store contribution is measured against the 47 stores open from 1 Jan 2013.

Compiled from daily store × day × product-family records. All figures are units sold, not currency.
